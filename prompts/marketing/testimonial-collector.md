# Testimonial Collector

> Real client quotes you can publish, collected without the awkward ask

**Category:** Marketing & Content · **Difficulty:** No-code · **Works with:** Claude, ChatGPT

## The problem

Your happiest clients would gladly vouch for you, and your website still says nothing because the ask feels awkward, the timing never seems right, and when someone does send praise it arrives as "you guys are great!!" which helps nobody. Google reviews have their own machinery (the [Review Reputation Manager](/toolbox/review-reputation-manager) runs it); testimonials are a different asset: the specific, attributable quote that closes a hesitant prospect on your website, proposal, or [case study](/toolbox/case-study-writer). Collecting them well is a small process, and this prompt runs it end to end.

## The prompt

```
You are helping a small business owner collect publishable
testimonials from happy clients, without awkwardness and without
putting words in anyone's mouth.

MY BUSINESS: [WHAT YOU SELL, TO WHOM]
THE CLIENT: [NAME, WHAT YOU DID FOR THEM, THE RESULT IF YOU KNOW
IT, AND THE RECENT MOMENT THAT MAKES NOW THE RIGHT TIME: A
THANK-YOU, A REORDER, A PROJECT WRAPPED]
WHERE IT WILL LIVE: [WEBSITE / PROPOSALS / A CASE STUDY / ALL]
WHAT PROSPECTS HESITATE ABOUT: [PRICE / TRUST / SWITCHING /
WHETHER IT WORKS FOR BUSINESSES LIKE THEIRS]

PRODUCE, IN ORDER:
1. THE ASK: A 60-word message in my voice, sent close to their
   happy moment, that asks for two minutes and makes declining
   easy. No forms, no "would you be willing to provide a
   testimonial" stiffness.
2. THE 4 QUESTIONS: Interview questions that pull specifics
   matched to what my prospects hesitate about ("what almost
   stopped you from hiring us?" beats "did you like the
   service?"). Askable by email or in one short call.
3. THE EDIT PASS: Take their raw answers [PASTE WHEN RECEIVED]
   and cut them into 1-3 publishable quotes: their words only,
   tightened for length, nothing invented, specifics kept.
   Flag anything that needs their approval beyond trimming.
4. THE APPROVAL NOTE: A two-line message showing them the final
   quote and how it will appear (name, business, photo or not),
   asking for a plain yes.

RULES: Never write a testimonial for the client to sign; the
words must start as theirs. Keep the numbers and specifics they
volunteer; those are the persuasive part. If their answers are
generic praise, give me one follow-up question to pull a
specific, not a rewrite that fakes one.
```

The timing line in the setup is the actual trick: the ask sent within a day of a thank-you or a reorder gets answered; the same ask sent cold in a "testimonial drive" gets politely ignored. Batch this prompt after every project wrap and the website section fills itself in a quarter.

## How to use it

1. Copy the full prompt into Claude or ChatGPT, one client at a time, right after a happy moment.
2. Send the ask the same day; the window is short.
3. When answers come back, paste them into step 3 and pick the quote that answers your prospects' actual hesitation.
4. Always run the approval note, exactly as worded; a testimonial someone half-remembers approving is a relationship risk, not an asset.
5. Store the approved quotes with their date and context; the [Case Study Writer](/toolbox/case-study-writer) turns the best one into the long version.

## Example

A bookkeeping practice wraps a painful cleanup project and the client emails "honestly you saved my year." The owner runs the prompt within the hour. The ask goes out referencing that exact line; the four questions include "what almost stopped you from switching bookkeepers?" The raw answers come back rambling but contain "I put it off for two years because I was embarrassed by the state of my books, and I shouldn't have." The edit pass keeps that sentence nearly whole, the approval note shows it with name and business, and the yes comes back in ten minutes. That quote now sits on the pricing page, doing more work than any paragraph the owner ever wrote about themselves.

## Pro tip

Collect against your objections, not your ego. The flattering quote ("so professional!") decorates; the objection-shaped quote ("I thought we were too small for this") converts, because it is the hesitating prospect hearing themselves. Keep a one-line list of your top three objections and aim each interview at one of them.
