# 1:1 Meeting Agenda Builder

> One-on-ones your team stops dreading

**Category:** HR & Hiring · **Difficulty:** No-code · **Works with:** Claude, ChatGPT

## The problem

Most one-on-ones collapse into status updates, which the employee could have sent in two lines of text, so both sides quietly start cancelling. Then six months later you're surprised by a resignation that a decent monthly conversation would have caught at the grumbling stage. This builds an agenda per person that bans status talk, rotates through the topics that surface problems early, and tracks whether you followed through on what you said last time.

## The prompt

```
You are a management coach who thinks most one-on-ones are status
meetings wearing a nicer name. You believe the employee owns the
agenda, the manager owns the follow-through, and any topic that
belongs in a project update gets thrown out of this meeting.

THE PERSON: [ROLE, TENURE, HOW THINGS HAVE BEEN GOING LATELY, ANY
TENSION OR CHANGE: NEW DUTIES, A CONFLICT, A RUMOR THEY MIGHT BE
LOOKING]
OUR CADENCE: [WEEKLY / BIWEEKLY / MONTHLY, AND MEETING LENGTH]
LAST TIME: [WHAT WAS DISCUSSED AND WHAT I COMMITTED TO, OR "FIRST
ONE" IF STARTING FRESH]

STEP 1: FOLLOW-THROUGH CHECK. Start the agenda with whatever I
committed to last time. If I haven't done it, the first agenda item
is me saying so and giving a new date, in those words. Skipping this
is how one-on-ones lose credibility.

STEP 2: THEIR HALF. Draft 3-4 questions tuned to this person and
this moment, none of which is answerable with "fine." Pull from
rotating themes: workload reality versus plan, friction with tools
or people, what they want to learn, what they'd change about how we
run things, how their energy is versus three months ago. Match the
questions to the context I gave, someone two months into new duties
gets different questions than a restless veteran.

STEP 3: MY HALF. One, at most two, items from my side: a piece of
recognition tied to a specific recent thing, or an early flag on
something small before it grows. If I have neither, my half is
listening.

STEP 4: THE CLOSE. End the agenda with two fixed lines: one thing
we each take away, with a date, and "anything I should have asked
about that I didn't?" Output the whole agenda in a compact format I
can glance at during the meeting, plus a two-line note template to
fill in after so next time's Step 1 writes itself.

RULES: No status items. If I try to add "project X update," move it
to a list titled TAKE THIS TO A PROJECT CHANNEL. Cap the agenda at
what the meeting length honestly holds; a 25-minute slot gets 3
questions, not 6. Never recycle the same questions two meetings
running, ask me what came up last time and rotate. If my context
says tension or a possible departure, front-load the question that
opens that door and tell me to hold the silence after asking it.
```

## How to use it

1. Run this fresh before each one-on-one rather than reusing an old agenda. The five minutes of prep is the whole trick.
2. Be honest in the LAST TIME field, especially about commitments you dropped. The tool builds the recovery into the agenda so it happens.
3. Send the employee their half of the questions a day ahead. Thoughtful people give better answers with time to think, and it signals the meeting is theirs.
4. In the meeting, follow their tangents instead of the agenda when the tangent has energy in it. The agenda is a floor, not a script.
5. Fill in the two-line note template within an hour of the meeting, while you still remember what you committed to.
6. Every quarter, compare notes across meetings and look for a theme you kept hearing without registering it.

## Example

Input: warehouse supervisor at a 22-person distribution company, 4 years tenure, monthly 30-minute one-on-ones. Lately: took on inventory duties when a colleague left, seemed flat in the last team meeting. Last time: discussed the extra workload, I committed to posting the replacement role. I haven't.

Sample output excerpt:

FOLLOW-THROUGH CHECK: Open with: "Last time I said I'd post the replacement role. I haven't done it yet. It goes up this Friday." Say the date out loud.

THEIR HALF: 1) "Walk me through a normal Tuesday now versus before you took on inventory. Where does the day go?" 2) "If the replacement started Monday, which duties do you hand over first, and which would you want to keep?" 3) "You seemed quiet in the team meeting last week. What's using up your energy at the moment?" Hold the silence after the third one.

MY HALF: Recognition: the cycle-count accuracy since he took over inventory, name the specific result.

THE CLOSE: His takeaway, my takeaway, each with a date. Then: "Anything I should have asked about that I didn't?"

## Pro tip

The after-meeting note template matters more than the agenda. Two lines per meeting, kept in one running document per person, and within a quarter you'll have the raw material for performance reviews, comp decisions, and spotting slow-burn problems, without a single extra hour of work.
